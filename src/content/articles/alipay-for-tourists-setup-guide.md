# Alipay for Tourists 2026: Step-by-Step Setup With a Foreign Card

If you only install one app before flying to China, make it Alipay. It is not just a wallet — it is the layer that sits over metro gates, taxi payments, museum tickets, convenience stores, food courts and half the vending machines in the country. A foreign credit card in your pocket will get you through hotel check-in and little else.

The good news: since 2023 Alipay accepts international Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Discover and Diners Club cards directly, with no need for a Chinese bank account. The less good news: identity verification trips up a meaningful share of first-time visitors, and the failure messages are unhelpful. This guide walks through the setup in the order that actually works, explains what the fees and limits really are, and covers the fixes for the errors tourists hit most often.

## Why Alipay and Not Just Your Credit Card?

China moved to QR-code payments years ago and never looked back. A typical day looks like this:

- **Breakfast**: CNY 12 (about US$1.70) for congee and youtiao — QR code on the wall
- **Metro**: gate scanner reads a QR code generated by your phone
- **Lunch**: CNY 45 noodle shop — you scan the code taped to the table
- **Taxi**: driver shows a QR code, you scan and type the fare
- **Museum**: timed-entry ticket bought in a mini program inside Alipay

Card terminals exist in international hotels, big shopping malls and airport shops. Everywhere else, the terminal is a printed square. Some vendors will accept cash, but they may have to hunt for change, and a few will look at you like you've handed them a fax machine.

Alipay is also the most foreigner-friendly of the two dominant wallets. WeChat Pay works, but its international card support is narrower and its verification is fussier. Set up Alipay as your primary and WeChat Pay as a backup only if you have time.

## What You Need Before You Start

Have these ready in one sitting — the setup goes much faster:

1. **Your passport** (the physical booklet, not a photo of the data page on your laptop)
2. **A mobile number you control**, ideally your home number, able to receive SMS
3. **A Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Discover or Diners Club card** — credit cards clear more reliably than debit cards for cross-border charges
4. **Your banking app** installed and logged in, so you can approve a suspicious-transaction prompt
5. **Good Wi-Fi** and 20 uninterrupted minutes

Do this at home, not at the arrivals hall. Verification sometimes needs a retry, and retrying while standing in a taxi queue with jet lag is miserable.

## Step 1: Install and Register

Download Alipay from the Apple App Store or Google Play. The app is published internationally, so you do not need a Chinese app store account.

Open it and choose **Sign Up**. Alipay accepts foreign numbers with the country code (+1, +44, +61, +49 and so on). You will get an SMS verification code. Use a number you will still have access to during the trip — this matters later, because your bank's 3-D Secure codes may arrive by SMS.

Once in, switch the language to English if it hasn't defaulted: **Me → Settings (gear icon) → General → Language**. The English interface is complete enough for payments, though some mini programs inside it are Chinese-only.

## Step 2: Complete Identity Verification

This is the step people abandon. Alipay requires identity verification before it will accept a foreign card, because international card payments are regulated as cross-border transactions.

Go to **Me → Account & Security → Identity Verification** (wording shifts slightly between versions). You will be asked to:

- Select **Passport** as your ID type
- Photograph the photo page — flat surface, no glare, all four corners visible, machine-readable zone at the bottom legible
- Take a selfie or short liveness video when prompted
- Enter your name **exactly as printed in the passport**, family name and given name in the correct fields

Two details cause most rejections. First, **name order**: if your passport shows "SMITH, JOHN ROBERT," the family name is Smith and the given names are John Robert — do not type "John Smith" into the surname field. Second, **middle names**: include them if they appear on the passport, because the card issuer will send the full name and a mismatch can cause a decline later.

Verification is usually approved within minutes. If it sits in "under review," it typically clears within 24 hours — another reason to do this days before departure rather than at the airport.

## Step 3: Link Your Card

With verification approved, go to **Me → Bank Cards → Add Card** (or tap the **+** on the wallet card area).

Enter the card number, expiry, CVV and the billing address exactly as your bank has it. The billing address matters: an address mismatch is a standard anti-fraud trigger on cross-border e-commerce, and Alipay passes this data to your issuer.

Most cards link instantly. You may be asked to complete 3-D Secure — a code by SMS, or an approval in your banking app. **This is the moment to check that your home SIM can still receive texts.** If you plan to swap to a local Chinese SIM on arrival, keep your home number alive via roaming for SMS, or set up app-based approval with your bank before you leave.

Add a second card if you have one. Cards get frozen by fraud systems surprisingly often when they suddenly start showing transactions in Shanghai, and having a backup already linked turns a 40-minute phone call into a two-tap fix.

## Step 4: Understand the Fees

Alipay's pricing for foreign cards is simple once you know where the fee sits:

- **Alipay does not normally add a service fee to you, the payer.** You pay the price on the menu.
- **Alipay charges the merchant** a processing fee on international-card transactions. The commonly quoted structure is roughly 3% on transactions of CNY 200 (about US$28) and above, with smaller transactions exempt. This is why some small vendors prefer cash or a local wallet for larger bills.
- **Your bank may add a foreign transaction fee**, typically 0–3% depending on the card. A no-foreign-transaction-fee card saves you real money over a two-week trip.

So a CNY 45 bowl of noodles costs CNY 45. A CNY 300 department-store purchase costs CNY 300 from your account, minus whatever your own bank charges. There is no hidden tourist surcharge — but do not expect the merchant's 3% to be invisible either; a handful of small businesses will ask you to cover it or quietly steer you to cash.

For reference, typical 2026 spending on a two-week trip: street food and casual meals CNY 1,500–2,500, metro and taxis CNY 400–700, attractions CNY 500–900, plus hotels and trains paid separately. That is roughly US$350–600 of QR-code spending, which is well within normal card limits.

## Step 5: Know the Limits

Alipay applies per-transaction and cumulative caps to international cards, and the exact numbers have been raised several times since 2023 and vary by card issuer, verification level and risk scoring. In practice:

- Everyday purchases — meals, metro, taxis, convenience stores — never come close to a limit.
- Mid-sized purchases such as a high-speed rail ticket or a hotel balance are usually fine.
- Very large single purchases (designer goods, expensive electronics, long hotel stays) may be declined. The workaround is to split the payment, pay with the physical card at the terminal, or use a different card.

Do not build a plan that depends on putting a US$2,000 purchase through a QR code. Use your card directly for that, and keep Alipay for the hundred small transactions that make up an actual trip.

## Step 6: Paying in the Real World

Two directions, and confusing them is the classic beginner error.

**You scan the merchant.** Tap **Scan** at the top of the home screen, point at the printed QR code, check that the merchant name and amount look right, confirm. This is what you do at restaurants, shops and market stalls.

**The merchant scans you.** Tap **Pay/Collect** (the barcode icon) to display your payment code. Cashiers at convenience stores, supermarkets and chains scan it with a handheld device. Never screenshot or share this code — it is effectively a signed blank cheque.

**Metro and buses.** In Alipay, open **Transport** and select your city. Most major Chinese cities — Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Chengdu, Hangzhou, Xi'an, Chongqing — issue a ride QR code that works at the gate. It saves you from buying a transit card and queueing at machines.

**Taxis and ride-hailing.** The built-in **Didi** mini program works in English, shows the fare upfront and pays through Alipay, so no cash changes hands. For street taxis, the driver will show a QR code on the back of the seat or their phone; scan it, type the meter amount, confirm.

**Refunds.** Card refunds on cross-border transactions are slow — often a few business days, sometimes up to two weeks. If a vendor refunds you in Alipay balance instead, that money can only be spent in China, which is useless on your last day. Ask for the refund back to the card.

## Troubleshooting: The Errors You Will Actually See

**"Payment failed — please contact your card issuer."** Your bank blocked the charge. Open your banking app, approve the transaction or the merchant, and retry. If nothing appears, call the number on the back of your card and tell them you are travelling in China.

**3-D Secure code never arrives.** The code went to your home number and you have no SMS. Switch on roaming for texts (receiving SMS is usually free), use your bank's app approval instead, or pay with a different card that uses app-based verification.

**"This merchant cannot accept this payment method."** You are scanning a **personal** QR code — a individuals' transfer code, common at tiny shops and market stalls — rather than a merchant code. International cards usually cannot pay those. Hand over cash.

**"Identity verification failed."** Re-shoot the passport page on a dark, flat surface in daylight, and re-check name order. Retry once; if it fails again, wait a few hours before trying, since repeated attempts can trigger a cooldown.

**Payment works, but the amount looks wrong.** Some merchants add a card-processing surcharge at the counter. Ask before paying — "shuā kǎ yào jiā qián ma?" (刷卡要加钱吗?) — and pay cash if the answer is yes.

**Phone dead at the wrong moment.** This is the real failure mode of a cashless trip. Carry a power bank, and keep CNY 200–500 in notes for the day your battery dies at 21:00 in a night market.

## Practical Habits That Save Trips

- **Set a screen lock and keep your phone in a front pocket.** Everything — money, maps, train tickets, hotel booking — lives on that device.
- **Download the app and finish verification a week before you fly**, not the night before.
- **Tell your bank you are travelling**, even if they say it is unnecessary.
- **Add both Alipay and a backup card**, and note your bank's international collect number somewhere other than your phone.
- **Use the Translate tool inside Alipay** to photograph Chinese menus and signs; it is adequate for ordering and surprisingly good with handwritten specials boards.
- **Keep your passport with you** — hotels, some ticket counters and a few attractions still require the physical document, and Alipay verification information must match it.

## Concluding

Alipay is the single highest-value 20 minutes of preparation for a China trip. Do the verification at home with your passport on a table, link a credit card and a backup, confirm your bank can reach you by SMS or app, and you will spend your holiday scanning codes like a local instead of hunting for ATMs.

The setup is not flawless — name mismatches, bank fraud blocks and personal QR codes will still catch some travellers — but every one of those failures has a straightforward workaround, and none of them require a Chinese bank account. Carry a little cash, keep a power bank, and treat Alipay as the default rather than the only option. That combination handles essentially everything China will throw at you, from a CNY 12 breakfast to a week of metro rides.
