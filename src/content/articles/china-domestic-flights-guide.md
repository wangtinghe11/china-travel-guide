# Domestic Flights in China: Booking, Airports, and the Mistakes Tourists Make

China's high-speed rail gets all the attention, and for journeys under about 1,200 km it genuinely wins. But the country also runs one of the largest domestic aviation markets on earth, and for the long diagonals — Beijing to Kunming, Shanghai to Urumqi, Guangzhou to Harbin — flying saves you a day.

The problem is that flying domestically in China is not quite the same ritual as flying at home. The booking channel matters, the airport code matters more than most visitors realise, and the baggage rules on the cheap fare you just found are probably not what you assumed. This guide covers the parts that actually trip people up.

## The Short Answer

Book direct with the airline's own site or app about two to four weeks out. Fly into the airport closest to your hotel, and read the three-letter code rather than the city name. Arrive 1.5 hours early — Beijing Capital's own passenger guide asks for that and warns that [check-in closes 30–45 minutes before departure](https://www.bcia.com.cn/lkzncf.html) — and add half an hour if you are at a mega-hub. Assume your low-cost fare includes no checked bag until you have read the fare rules.

## When Flying Beats the Train (and When It Doesn't)

The rule of thumb that works: if the high-speed rail journey is under five hours, take the train. Beijing–Shanghai is 4.5 hours city-centre to city-centre, and the airport version of that trip eats four hours in transfers, security and boarding on top of the two-hour flight.

Flying wins in three situations:

- **Distance.** Anything over roughly 1,500 km. Shanghai–Chengdu by rail is 11–12 hours; the flight is three.
- **Geography.** West China is enormous and sparsely served by rail. Lhasa, Urumqi, Dunhuang and Jiuzhaigou are flight destinations first and rail destinations second.
- **Small cities with small airports.** Zhangjiajie's Hehua airport is ten minutes from the city and almost empty — a much calmer arrival than the three-hour rail-plus-bus combination from Changsha.

And flying loses badly in one specific case: Chinese public holidays. During Golden Week (early October) and Spring Festival, domestic fares can triple while trains sell out weeks ahead. If your dates overlap a national holiday, the train is not just cheaper, it is the only thing that will still be available.

## Booking: Four Channels, Four Different Experiences

**1. The airline's own English website.** China Southern and Spring Airlines both run full English booking sites — China Southern's [English portal](https://www.csair.com/en/) handles booking, check-in and refunds rather than just displaying a brochure, and Spring's [English site](https://en.ch.com/) sells and manages its own low-cost fares. This is the channel to use when something goes wrong: a delay, a cancellation, a name correction. Third-party agencies cannot fix your ticket; the airline can.

**2. Chinese booking apps.** Ctrip (携程) and its international arm Trip.com are the same company. Trip.com is genuinely convenient for foreigners and accepts foreign cards without drama, but it adds a service fee and its customer service is a layer between you and the airline.

**3. Airline apps in Chinese.** Cheapest, and the only place some promotional fares exist. Worth it if you can navigate it; not worth spending a holiday fighting a Chinese-language captcha.

**4. At the airport counter.** The fallback when your card fails online, at the full flexible fare. Walk-up domestic prices in China are brutal.

Two booking details that cause real problems:

- **Name order.** Chinese systems want the surname first, exactly as printed in the passport's machine-readable zone. "Mary Jane Smith" booked as given-name-first will sometimes fail at the counter. Copy the passport, do not retype from memory.
- **Passport validity and visa.** The airline checks that you have a valid entry document for China when you board an international flight, but on a purely domestic flight your passport alone is the ID. No visa check happens at the domestic gate.

## Two-Airport Cities: The Mistake That Ruins Day One

This is the single most expensive mistake in Chinese domestic travel, and it is not rare — the airport codes differ by one letter and the airports are 60 km apart.

**Beijing.** Capital (PEK) is northeast of the city, 25 minutes to Dongzhimen on the Airport Express for about CNY 25. Daxing (PKX) is far south, an hour or more from the centre, served by its own express line to Caoqiao. Most international long-haul lands at PEK; a lot of domestic and some international traffic now runs through PKX. If you land at one and depart from the other, budget 90 minutes minimum and do not book a tight connection.

**Shanghai.** Hongqiao (SHA) is the close one — about 30 minutes by Metro Line 2 or 10, and it shares a complex with Hongqiao Railway Station, which is how you do a same-day flight-to-train transfer. Pudong (PVG) handles most international arrivals and is 40–60 minutes out by taxi, or 8 minutes to Longyang Road on the Maglev for CNY 50 plus a metro transfer.

**Chengdu.** Shuangliu (CTU) sits inside the city ring; Tianfu (TFU) is a vast new airport an hour away on Metro Line 18. Most international flights now use TFU. Booking a hotel near "Chengdu airport" without checking which one is a classic first-night disaster.

**Xi'an.** One airport (Xianyang, XIY) but it is 40 km northwest of the city. Metro Line 14 now connects it; the airport bus is about CNY 25 and drops at several downtown points.

**Chongqing.** Jiangbei (CKG), Metro Line 10, roughly 40 minutes to the centre. The airport bus is around CNY 20.

**Shenzhen.** Bao'an (SZX) looks like a spaceship landed on the coast, and Metro Line 11 gets you to Futian in about 30 minutes.

**Hangzhou.** Xiaoshan (HGH), Metro Lines 1 and 19, around 40 minutes.

**Guangzhou.** Baiyun (CAN), Metro Line 3, and one of the better-organised big terminals in the country.

## Airport Logistics: Trains, Taxis and the Touts

Every major Chinese airport has a rail link, and it is almost always the right answer. Airport expresses are cheap (CNY 25–50), signposted in English, and immune to traffic. The exception is heavy luggage to somewhere not on the line — then a taxi or Didi is worth the CNY 100–150.

Use the official taxi rank, which is always signposted and always a physical queue. Inside the arrivals hall you will be approached by men asking "taxi? taxi?" — these are not taxis, and the fare is whatever they think you will pay. Walk past them to the rank.

Didi (the Chinese equivalent of Uber, available in English inside Alipay) is the transparent option: the fare is fixed before you accept, and there is no argument about the meter. The pickup point is the tricky part — Chinese airports have designated ride-hailing zones, often on a specific level of a specific car park, and the app's pin will not always match where the driver actually waits. Follow the signs for 网约车 (ride-hailing) rather than the taxi signs.

## Check-In, Security and the Baggage Rules That Differ

Chinese domestic security is stricter than what most Western travellers are used to, and slower because of it. Laptops and power banks come out of the bag, liquids get looked at, and belts and shoes sometimes come off. The queues move, but they move at Chinese speed, which is to say efficiently and without much patience for people who have not read the rules.

Baggage is where the money is. On a full-service carrier, the standard economy allowance on a domestic ticket is around 20 kg checked, with the cabin bag limited to roughly 5 kg and 20×40×55 cm — Beijing Capital publishes exactly these figures in its [departure guide](https://www.bcia.com.cn/lkzncf.html). On a low-cost carrier the numbers are much tighter: Spring Airlines gives you one 7 kg cabin bag at 30×20×40 cm on its base fare and **no checked luggage at all**, with 20 kg only appearing on the higher fare tier, as set out in its [excess baggage policy](https://en.ch.com/flights/excess-baggage). A CNY 400 fare plus CNY 300 of airport excess-baggage charges is not a CNY 400 fare.

Rule of thumb: weigh your bag at the hotel before you leave. Chinese airports charge excess baggage by the kilogram at the counter, and the rate is not kind.

## The Power Bank Rule Nobody Warns You About

Since 28 June 2025, Chinese airports have enforced a rule that power banks carried on domestic flights must show a valid CCC (3C) certification mark on the casing, and recalled models are banned outright. Hangzhou Airport published the [CAAC notice](https://www.hzairport.com/en/notice/detail/id/120.html) on its own site. Unmarked banks — including most of the ones sold in Western airports and many older Anker and generic units — get pulled at security and confiscated, with no compensation and no appeal.

If you are flying domestically inside China, bring a power bank that visibly carries the CCC logo, or plan to buy one after arrival (they are everywhere and cheap). Do not put a power bank in checked luggage under any circumstances; that is a separate and universal prohibition.

## Transiting Without a Visa

If you are using China's visa-free transit rather than a tourist visa, domestic flights change the arithmetic. The 24-hour visa-free transit rule, as described by the [National Immigration Administration](https://en.nia.gov.cn/n147418/n147463/c183412/content.html), applies at all open ports to nationals of all countries, but only for passengers with a confirmed onward ticket to a **third** country or region, and it does not permit leaving the port's restricted area without a temporary entry permit.

In plain terms: a same-day connection through Shanghai to Bangkok is fine. Landing in Shanghai, flying domestically to Chengdu, then leaving from Chengdu is not a 24-hour transit — it is entry, and entry needs the 240-hour transit policy or a visa. Check your exact routing against the current rules before you book the domestic leg, because the airline will check it at the international boarding gate.

## Delays, Cancellations and What You Can Actually Claim

Chinese domestic aviation is punctual by global standards in clear weather, and badly affected by it in bad weather: summer thunderstorms in the south and east, winter fog in the north and in the Sichuan basin, and the occasional air-traffic-flow restriction that grounds everything for two hours with no explanation.

What to do when it happens:

- **Go to the airline counter, not the agency.** Rebooking authority lives with the carrier.
- **Domestic delays do not trigger cash compensation** under Chinese rules for weather or flow control — you get rebooking, a meal voucher after a few hours and a hotel if it is an overnight. Do not expect EU261.
- **Travel insurance is the only real protection**, and it needs to cover "travel delay" specifically, not just cancellation.
- **Book the long-haul home leg with a buffer.** A same-day domestic-to-international connection in China is a gamble; overnighting near the international airport before your flight home costs one hotel night and removes a whole category of disaster.

## A Practical Checklist Before You Fly

- Read the three-letter airport code, then check the airport's distance to your hotel.
- Confirm the passport name order matches the booking, character for character.
- Check the fare type: cabin bag only, or cabin plus checked.
- Weigh your bag; know your carrier's excess rate.
- Confirm your power bank has a CCC mark.
- Screenshot the booking and the airport-express route — airport Wi-Fi often wants a Chinese phone number to log in.
- Arrive 1.5 hours early, two hours at PKX, PVG or TFU.
- Carry CNY 100–200 in small notes for the taxi fallback.

None of this is exotic. It is just the local version of the routine, and the travellers who have a bad first day in China are almost always the ones who assumed the routine was the same everywhere.
