# Getting Cash in China: ATMs, Exchange, and Fees (2026)

China runs on QR codes, but cash has not disappeared — and the moment your phone dies, your bank freezes your card, or a village noodle shop hands you a personal transfer code instead of a merchant code, cash saves the day. The problem is that almost nothing about getting cash in China works the way a first-time visitor expects: ATMs cap you low, fees are per-transaction rather than per-yuan, and the machine will cheerfully offer to convert your money at a rate that costs you 5%.

This guide covers the mechanics: which ATMs take foreign cards, what a withdrawal actually costs, when an exchange counter beats an ATM, and how much renminbi you need in 2026.

## The Short Answer

- **Use ATMs, not exchange counters, for everyday top-ups.** A flat fee of CNY 15–30 on a CNY 3,000 withdrawal is under 1%.
- **Withdraw in CNY, never in your home currency.** Decline every "convert to USD/EUR/GBP?" prompt.
- **Take out large amounts, rarely.** Two withdrawals of CNY 3,000 cost less than six of CNY 500.
- **Carry CNY 300–500 in notes** as a personal buffer and let ATMs refill you as you travel.
- **Get cash during the day, at a bank branch, in a city.** Airport and rural ATMs are where things go wrong.

## What Your Withdrawal Really Costs

Three separate charges stack on top of each other.

**1. The Chinese ATM operator fee.** Most banks charge a flat CNY 15–30 for foreign-card withdrawals, and a few add roughly 1% of the amount. Bank of China is usually the cheapest and most reliable for foreign cards; ICBC and China Construction Bank are widespread but their fees vary.

**2. Your own bank's fee.** Typically US$3–5 per overseas ATM use, or a percentage. Some travel accounts and neobanks charge nothing — check before you fly, because this is the one fee you can eliminate.

**3. The exchange rate spread.** This is the invisible cost. If you let the ATM convert to your home currency (dynamic currency conversion, or DCC), the machine uses a retail rate 3–7% worse than the interbank rate. The prompt sounds helpful — "guaranteed rate, no surprises" — but it is one of the most expensive clicks in travel. Always select **CNY / RMB / 人民币** and let your bank convert. The daily reference rates are published on [Bank of China's RMB exchange rate board](https://www.boc.cn/sourcedb/whpj/enindex_1619.html) — worth a glance before you fly so you know what a fair rate looks like.

Worked example: you need CNY 3,000. One withdrawal at a CNY 15 operator fee plus a US$5 home-bank fee costs roughly US$440. Six withdrawals of CNY 500 cost about US$455 and twenty minutes at ATM screens — and accepting DCC at 5% on each loses you another US$20.

## The ATM Routine That Works

Step by step, in the order that avoids most failures:

1. **Find a machine attached to a bank branch.** Bank of China, ICBC, China Construction Bank, Bank of Communications and China Merchants Bank are the reliable names for foreign cards. Airport and convenience-store ATMs also work, but a branch machine is far less likely to be out of cash or fitted with a skimmer.
2. **Check the logos.** Visa, Mastercard, Cirrus or Plus must be printed on the machine or shown on screen. UnionPay-only machines will reject your card — [UnionPay's visitor guide](https://www.unionpayintl.com/dynamic/ExploreMainlandChinaYourWay/en) notes that its network covers over a million ATMs in mainland China, but that is UnionPay cards, not your Visa.
3. **Insert the card and choose English.** Almost all bank ATMs have an English menu, usually labelled "EN".
4. **Choose "Cash Withdrawal", not "Balance Enquiry".** Balance enquiries are often charged as a separate transaction by your home bank.
5. **Decline the conversion prompt.** You will be asked whether to withdraw in CNY or your home currency. Pick CNY.
6. **Enter an amount in multiples of CNY 100.** Machines dispense CNY 100 notes and cap withdrawals at CNY 3,000 for cards issued outside mainland China. Asking for CNY 3,500 will be rejected.
7. **Take the card first, then the cash, then the receipt.** Chinese ATMs return the card before dispensing notes, which trips up people who walk away at the wrong moment. Keep the receipt — it is the only record if the machine debits you and gives nothing.

If the machine rejects you twice, stop. Repeated attempts trigger your bank's fraud system and the lockout can take a phone call to clear. Try another bank's ATM.

## Exchanging Cash: Airport Counter vs Bank vs ATM

China's foreign-exchange rules were tightened up for visitors in 2024, when the State Council issued guidance requiring airports, ports and hotels to add currency-exchange facilities and pressuring banks to keep [cash services and foreign-card ATM access working](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202403/content_6937624.htm). In practice you now have four options:

| Option | Rate | Speed | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| City bank counter | Best | 20–40 min, passport required | Large amounts, odd currencies |
| ATM | Fair, plus flat fee | 3 min | Everyday top-ups |
| Airport exchange counter | 2–5% worse | 5 min | Emergency arrival cash |
| Hotel front desk | Worst | Instant | Last-resort only |

At a bank counter you need your passport, and staff may ask what the money is for. Individual conversion of foreign currency into renminbi runs under an annual facility capped at the equivalent of USD 50,000, as [Bank of China's personal exchange documentation](https://www.boc.cn/en/pbservice/pb4/200806/t20080626_1323981.html) sets out — irrelevant for a tourist, but it explains why the process feels more formal than at home.

One rule that catches people out: **never exchange money on the street.** The rates are terrible and counterfeit CNY 100 notes circulate. Use a bank counter or an ATM, where the notes are checked.

## Where You'll Actually Need Cash, City by City

This is where the generic advice falls apart. After a few days in China you will notice that cashlessness is city-by-city, not country-wide.

- **Hangzhou** is Alipay's home city and effectively cashless — even donation boxes run on QR codes. Carry a token CNY 100 and expect to use it almost never.
- **Beijing** is close behind: the metro runs on Alipay's ride code, and the CNY 2 ticket for Jingshan Park's view over the Forbidden City is one of the few places cash still feels natural. Airport taxis run CNY 100–150; use Didi instead.
- **Chongqing** is the opposite extreme. Noodle shops charging CNY 7–10 a bowl, yellow taxis and the old stairways around Baixiangju are cash-friendly, and CNY 100 goes a long way.
- **Chengdu** is cash-light downtown but cash-useful at skewer restaurants, where the bill is counted in sticks and small notes settle the argument faster than a QR code.
- **Xi'an** and **Guilin** both have rental counters — city-wall bicycles in Xi'an, scooters in Yangshuo at CNY 30–50 a day — that often want a cash deposit.
- **Guangzhou** dim sum houses (CNY 60–100 per person) take cards fine, but the old dessert shops and roast-meat counters are frequently cash-first.
- **Shenzhen** is the most digital city in the country: Huaqiangbei electronics stalls prefer QR, but a cash offer at 50% of asking price closes deals faster than any app.
- **Shanghai** and **Zhangjiajie** sit in the middle: Shanghai is nearly cashless above ground, though taxis and breakfast stalls appreciate notes; Zhangjiajie's ticket windows (CNY 228 for a four-day park pass) move faster with a QR code than with a cashier counting change.

The pattern: the newer and richer the district, the less cash matters; the older the neighbourhood and the smaller the business, the more it does.

## The CNY 100 Note Problem

Chinese cash has one practical quirk no guide mentions: **small shops often cannot break a CNY 100 note, especially early in the morning.** A CNY 6 bowl of noodles paid with CNY 100 turns into five minutes of a cashier hunting through a drawer, and in a busy breakfast shop you will be waved toward the QR code.

The fix is to break your money deliberately. Buy a bottle of water at a convenience store, pay with CNY 100 and pocket the change — that is how locals keep CNY 10s and 20s. Coins exist (CNY 0.1, 0.5 and 1) but are half-forgotten outside supermarkets; don't hand a taxi driver a fistful of them.

## Cash Rules, Refusals and Your Rights

Renminbi cash is legal tender, and since the 2024 State Council guidance, merchants in tourist areas, transport hubs, hospitals and major shopping districts are explicitly required to accept it and to keep change on hand. A shop that flatly refuses cash is breaking the rules, not exercising a preference. You can report it on **12363**, the People's Bank of China's financial consumer hotline — though in practice, showing the cash and waiting usually solves it.

The genuine exceptions are structural, not rude: unmanned stores, some app-only coffee chains and any mini-program checkout cannot take physical money. That is why the sensible setup is Alipay or WeChat Pay as your default, with CNY 300–500 as the fallback rather than the plan.

## Scams, Skimming and Counterfeits

- **Skimming.** Use ATMs inside bank branch lobbies, not freestanding machines in tourist markets. Cover the keypad, and if the card slot looks loose or has a plastic collar, walk away.
- **"The machine ate my card."** Chinese ATMs do sometimes retain foreign cards. Do not leave; go into the branch with your passport. If the branch is closed, call your bank to block the card and switch to a backup.
- **Counterfeit notes.** The classic scam is a taxi driver or vendor swapping your genuine CNY 100 for a fake and claiming you paid with it. Pay with notes straight from an ATM or bank, and use ride-hailing instead of street taxis at night.
- **The "no change" overcharge.** A vendor who cannot break CNY 100 may simply keep the change. Have small notes, or pay the exact amount.
- **Fake helpline stickers.** A sticker on an ATM advertising a "24-hour English helpline" is a red flag; banks do not put phone numbers on machines for you to call about a failed withdrawal.

## How Much Cash to Carry and in What Notes

For a one-week trip to major cities, **CNY 300–500 per person** is a comfortable buffer. Take it as two or three CNY 100 notes plus a spread of CNY 20s and 10s, and top up from an ATM rather than carrying a thick wallet.

Increase it if you are heading somewhere with fewer bank branches: rural Guangxi around Yangshuo, the villages below Longji's rice terraces, Zhangjiajie's Wulingyuan hotel strip, or any homestay that quotes prices in cash. There, CNY 800–1,000 is sensible — but break the notes before you leave the city.

Decrease it to almost nothing if your itinerary is Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen and Hangzhou. Those four cities let you pay for a week with a phone and a backup card, and the cash comes home unused.

And when you leave: any cash over the equivalent of USD 5,000 must be declared to customs on the way in or out. You can convert leftover renminbi back at an airport counter, but expect a poor rate and a receipt requirement — better to spend it than change it twice.

## Bottom Line

Cash in China is no longer the main way to pay, but it still keeps trips from derailing. Treat an ATM as your refill station: use a bank-branch machine, take CNY 3,000 at a time, always choose CNY over your home currency, and keep the receipt. Carry a few hundred yuan in mixed notes and break the big ones early.

The travellers who get stuck are rarely the ones who forgot cash — they are the ones who let the ATM convert their money, or who discovered at 22:00 in a Chongqing noodle shop that their only payment method was a phone at 2% battery.
