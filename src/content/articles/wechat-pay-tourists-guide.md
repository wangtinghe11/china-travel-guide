# WeChat Pay for Tourists: Link Your Card and Pay Like a Local

WeChat Pay is the payment app most tourists set up second, use reluctantly, and then discover they needed all along. Alipay is the easier on-ramp for a foreign card — but WeChat is the app your hotel, your restaurant, the railway and half the attractions actually run on. When a Chengdu noodle shop taped a QR code to the table, that code was as likely to be Weixin as Alipay.

This guide covers registering an account that survives WeChat's security checks, getting passport verification approved the first time, what the fees and limits really are, and — the part almost no guide mentions — what WeChat Pay genuinely cannot do with a foreign card.

## Why Bother When Alipay Already Works?

**Some merchants only display one code.** A restaurant that printed its Weixin merchant code on the wall is not going to produce an Alipay one because you asked nicely. Asking "wēixìn háishi zhīfùbǎo?" (微信还是支付宝?) works, but a second wallet answers faster than a translation app.

**Mini programs live inside WeChat.** China delivers services as lightweight apps nested in WeChat: the official 12306 train booking mini program, Didi ride-hailing, Meituan and Dianping for restaurants, city metro transit codes, and attraction booking systems. Shared links from a hotel, guide or Chinese friend almost always open in WeChat.

The official WeChat site describes the app as chats, calls and "life services" in one place, which is exactly the overlap that makes it useful for payments ([wechat.com](https://www.wechat.com/en/)). WeChat Pay's platform documentation is written for merchants, but it is the authoritative place to check network coverage: 49 countries and regions, 16+ settlement currencies, and QR, in-app and mini-program payments ([pay.weixin.qq.com](https://pay.weixin.qq.com/index.php/public/wechatpay_en)).

## Before You Start: The Account Problem Nobody Warns You About

Alipay will let a stranger with a passport and a Visa card set up an account alone, at midnight, in an airport. WeChat is fussier, and the friction is at registration, not payment.

When you create a WeChat account on a new phone, the app may run a **security verification**: it shows a QR code and asks an existing WeChat user to scan it and confirm they know you. That user typically needs a non-new account. With no WeChat-using contacts, you can be stuck at the door with no support channel to appeal to.

Practical rules that follow:

- **Register at home, weeks before you fly**, on the phone you will travel with.
- **Line up a verifier** — a colleague, friend or tour operator who has had WeChat for a while — and ask in advance.
- **Do not factory-reset or swap phones** mid-trip. Logging in on a new device triggers another verification round.
- **Keep the same phone number active.** Registering with a number you abandon in China is the most common way people lose their wallet.

WeChat also distinguishes the international app ("WeChat") from the mainland build ("Weixin"). The international app supports mainland payments once a card is linked; you do not need a Chinese app-store account.

## Step 1: Register and Switch the App to English

Download WeChat from the Apple App Store or Google Play, sign up with your home mobile number (country codes +1, +44, +61, +49 all work), and complete the SMS code.

Then make the app readable: **Me → Settings → General → Language → English**. Some menus still carry machine-translated labels and mini programs are frequently Chinese-only, but the account and wallet settings become navigable — which is what matters for setup. Set a strong password and enable the app lock; this account will hold payment credentials, chat history and booking records.

## Step 2: Open the Wallet and Verify Your Passport

WeChat Pay appears as **Services** (or **Pay**) on the Me tab once your account is in good standing. Inside, **Wallet** holds cards and balance.

Before any foreign card links, WeChat requires real-name verification — the same regulatory logic as Alipay, since cross-border card payments are regulated cross-border transactions. You will:

- Choose **Passport** as the document type
- Photograph the photo page flat, in daylight, all four corners in frame, machine-readable zone legible
- Complete a short face scan or liveness check
- Type your name exactly as printed, family name and given names in the correct fields

Two failure modes cause most rejections. **Name order**: "SMITH, JOHN ROBERT" means surname Smith, given names John Robert — not "John Smith" in the surname box. **Middle names**: include them when they appear on the passport, because your issuer transmits the full name and an inconsistent name raises the chance of a decline later.

Approval is usually minutes, occasionally up to 24 hours. Do it days before departure, not in the arrivals hall on airport Wi-Fi.

## Step 3: Link Your Visa or Mastercard

In **Wallet → Bank Cards → Add a Bank Card**, enter the card number, expiry, CVV and billing address exactly as your bank holds it. The billing address is not decoration: it is a standard anti-fraud signal on cross-border e-commerce, and WeChat passes it to your issuer.

WeChat Pay accepts international **Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Discover and Diners Club** cards. Most link instantly; some trigger 3-D Secure, either an SMS code or an approval inside your banking app.

That SMS step is where travellers get stranded. If you plan to buy a Chinese SIM on arrival, keep your home number alive for receiving texts (incoming SMS while roaming is normally free), or switch your bank to in-app approval before you leave. Add a **second card** too — fraud systems freeze cards with sudden activity in Shanghai more often than you would expect, and a pre-linked backup turns a 40-minute phone call into a two-tap fix.

## Step 4: The Four Ways You Will Actually Pay

Confusing these flows is the classic beginner error.

**Merchant scans you.** Open **Services → Pay/Receive** (收付款) to display a barcode and QR code. Convenience stores, supermarkets, chains and hotel desks scan it with a handheld device. The code refreshes every minute and is effectively a signed blank cheque — never screenshot it, never send it to anyone.

**You scan the merchant.** Tap **+ → Scan** and point at the printed code on the wall, table or counter. Check the merchant name and amount before confirming. This is the flow for noodle shops, market stalls and taxi drivers holding up a phone.

**Inside a mini program.** Restaurant ordering is increasingly a mini program: scan the table code, a menu opens, you order and pay without a human. This is where WeChat beats Alipay in daily use.

**Transit codes.** City metros issue ride codes through WeChat mini programs — Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Chengdu, Hangzhou, Xi'an and Chongqing all have a route in. Coverage differs by city, so read the signage rather than assuming.

## What WeChat Pay Cannot Do With a Foreign Card

- **No balance top-ups.** Top-ups require a mainland Chinese bank card; your foreign card is a direct merchant-payment instrument only.
- **No person-to-person transfers.** If a Chinese friend pays for your dinner, settle up in cash.
- **No red packets (hongbao).** They come out of balance.
- **No balance refunds.** A foreign-card refund returns to the card in one to fifteen business days. Ask for it to the card, or the money strands in an app you cannot spend from.
- **Nothing requiring a Chinese ID.** Most mini programs and all financial products want a mainland ID number, not a passport.

Budget CNY 200–500 in cash alongside the app. That is not a workaround, it is policy: the State Council's 2024 opinion on improving payment services requires key venues — attractions, hotels, hospitals, transport hubs — to accept mobile payment, bank cards **and** cash, and keeps cash as the explicit fallback ([gov.cn, 国办发〔2024〕10号](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202403/content_6937624.htm)). Refusing cash is a reportable violation, not house rules.

## Fees and Limits, Without the Myths

- **You pay the menu price.** WeChat does not add a tourist surcharge.
- **The merchant pays the processing fee** on international-card payments, commonly reported at around 3% on transactions above CNY 200, with smaller payments exempt. This is why a few small shops quietly prefer cash for large bills.
- **Your bank may add 0–3%** in foreign transaction fees. A no-foreign-fee card is worth real money over two weeks.

Reported ceilings are generous — single payments in the thousands of US dollars, annual caps in the tens of thousands — and have risen repeatedly since 2023. Do not plan around them. The binding constraints are the limit in your wallet, your issuer's fraud rules, and whether the merchant accepts international cards at all. A CNY 6,500 handbag is better bought with the physical card; a week of noodles, metro rides and taxis is nowhere near a limit.

## The Mini Program Layer: Trains, Museums and Tables

- **Trains.** The official 12306 mini program exists inside WeChat. Passport registration works but the English interface is thin; many travellers book through Trip.com and collect with a passport at the station.
- **Attractions.** Beijing's Palace Museum releases tickets through its official mini program and website, typically seven days ahead, and closes on Mondays — there are no on-site ticket sales, so the booking channel is not optional.
- **Restaurants.** Table QR codes open ordering mini programs; in Guangzhou's dim sum halls and Chengdu's hotpot places this is now the default.
- **Ride-hailing.** Didi's mini program runs in English, shows the fare up front and charges through WeChat.
- **Translation.** Long-press a message or photo and choose Translate. Crude for poetry, adequate for menus.

## City Notes From Our Own Trips

- **Beijing.** The Forbidden City sells nothing at the gate; book seven days out and keep the passport you registered with. On a Monday, swap it for Jingshan Park (CNY 2) and look down at the roofs instead.
- **Shanghai.** Shengjian baos are ordered by the liang — four pieces — and one liang is a snack, not a meal.
- **Chengdu.** Heming Teahouse in People's Park still runs on cover-charge tea and a cash tip for the ear cleaner; small notes help even where QR codes are everywhere.
- **Chongqing.** A bowl of xiaomian is CNY 7–10, and the hills drain your battery faster than the payments do. Carry a power bank: a dead phone here means no map, no metro code and no wallet.
- **Guilin.** Rice noodles cost CNY 5–8; pay first, then add the soup at the counter yourself.

## Troubleshooting: The Six Errors You Will See

**"Payment failed — contact your card issuer."** Your bank blocked the charge. Approve it in your banking app, or call the number on the card and say you are in China.

**The 3-D Secure code never arrives.** It went to a home number with no signal. Enable roaming for SMS, switch to in-app approval, or use your second card.

**"This merchant cannot accept this payment method."** You scanned a personal transfer code, not a merchant code. Small shops and stalls often use personal codes that cannot take international cards. Pay cash.

**"Please complete identity verification."** The passport check failed or was never finished. Re-shoot the photo page on a dark flat surface in daylight, fix the name order, and retry after a few hours — repeated attempts can trigger a cooldown.

**Your payment code will not scan.** The barcode refreshes; a stale screen or low brightness defeats the scanner. Turn brightness up and reopen the code.

**Wallet shows no cards after linking.** Usually a region or verification-state issue rather than a card problem. Confirm verification is approved and the app is updated.

## The Short Version

Set up Alipay first, WeChat Pay second, and treat neither as sufficient alone. Register WeChat weeks ahead on the phone you will travel with, line up a verifier for the security check, verify your passport with the name exactly as printed, and link two cards before you leave home. Expect the wallet to pay merchants only — no balance, no transfers, no red packets — and carry a few hundred yuan for the moments when a personal QR code, a flat battery or a cash-only teahouse gets in the way.

Get those basics right and WeChat stops being the app you reluctantly install and becomes the one that books your train, orders your dinner and gets you through the metro gate. That is what paying like a local means in China: not one perfect app, but two wallets, a little cash, and a charged phone.
