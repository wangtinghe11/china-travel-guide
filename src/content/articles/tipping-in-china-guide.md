# Tipping in China: When, Where, and How Much

You finish a good meal in Chengdu, leave CNY 30 on the table and walk out. The waitress chases you down the street to give it back. That scene plays out every week in China, and it explains tipping faster than any etiquette guide: it is not rudeness that stops people tipping, it is that the service economy is not built around it.

China pays service staff a wage and prices service into the bill. There is no tip line on a receipt and no jar by the counter. Add a country that runs on QR codes, and the real question is not "how much should I tip" but "in the few situations where a tip is normal, how do I hand it over" — plus one thing no travel site mentions: a guide asking you for a tip here is breaking the law.

## The Short Answer

- **Restaurants, cafes, bars, bakeries:** no tip. Ever.
- **Taxis and ride-hailing:** no tip. Round up only to skip coins.
- **Hotel porters, housekeeping:** yes, small. CNY 10–20.
- **Private tour guides and drivers:** yes, meaningfully. CNY 50–100 per person per day.
- **Spas and massage:** yes, CNY 20–50, discreetly.
- **Hair salons, delivery riders, metro staff:** no.

Carry CNY 100–200 in small notes for porters, room attendants, guides and drivers. Everything else, pay exactly what the bill says.

## Why Tipping Never Took Root Here

**Service is priced in.** Menus quote the full price including service. Staff are salaried, and in big cities a restaurant job often comes with accommodation and meals — so a tip reads as an odd gift and gets refused.

**Cashlessness removed the mechanics.** China moved to QR payments faster than any other large economy. There is no tip line on a card slip and no cash in most pockets.

**Tipping has a bad reputation.** For two decades the worst actors in Chinese tourism — commission-driven shopping stops, "free" tours that trapped visitors in jade showrooms — were paid through kickbacks and tip pressure. Tipping became associated with being scammed, and regulators responded. That history is why the law is unusually explicit.

## The Legal Line: A Guide May Not Ask

China's **Tourism Law** (中华人民共和国旅游法) is blunt about this. Article 41 requires guides to wear their licence, follow the itinerary, and states they may not solicit tips from tourists, nor induce, deceive or coerce tourists into shopping or extra paid activities. Article 35 bans agencies from luring customers with unrealistically cheap tours and profiting through kickbacks from shopping stops. The penalty for soliciting a tip is in Article 102: the guide must return the money and is fined CNY 1,000–10,000, licence suspended or revoked in serious cases — see the [full text on the Ministry of Culture and Tourism site](https://zwgk.mct.gov.cn/zfxxgkml/zcfg/fl/202105/t20210526_924763.html).

The distinction matters on a tour:

- **You may tip a guide voluntarily.** Nothing in the law stops you, and freelance guides who look after you well are grateful.
- **A guide may not demand, hint at, or engineer a tip.** "The driver works so hard, everyone usually gives 200" is not a friendly nudge, it is the behaviour the article was written to stop.
- **A guide may not take you shopping for commission.** If your "included" tour stops at a jade factory, that is the kickback model Article 35 prohibits.

Which is why the best-value guiding is booked directly: a licensed private guide at CNY 400–800 a day, paid up front, has no incentive to march you through a showroom.

## Where a Tip Is Genuinely Expected

The list is short — mostly jobs that handle your bags or work a long private day for you.

**Hotel porters.** CNY 10–20 a bag, on room delivery. Customary at a five-star hotel; at a chain budget hotel the desk carries the bag and a tip is unnecessary.

**Housekeeping.** CNY 10–20 a day, on the desk with a note. The most appreciated tip in China: room attendants are among the lowest-paid staff in the building and rarely see one. Leave it daily — whoever cleaned on day one is not the person who cleans on day four.

**Guides and drivers.** CNY 50–100 per person for a good day's guiding, up to CNY 200 if they also booked tickets, wrangled transport and translated all day. A driver doing six hours of mountain road daily for a week is working harder than the guide. Coach tours run on the domestic convention of CNY 20–30 a head.

**Spa and massage therapists.** Handed to the therapist, not the desk, where it may go into a pool.

**Mountain porters.** In Zhangjiajie, Huangshan and the Guilin karst, porters carry supplies up steps no vehicle can reach. Cash is the only way to pay.

## How Much, Concretely

| Situation | Amount |
|---|---|
| Restaurant, any tier | CNY 0 |
| Taxi / Didi | CNY 0 |
| Hotel porter | CNY 10–20 per bag |
| Housekeeping | CNY 10–20 per day |
| Private day guide | CNY 50–100 per person |
| Multi-day driver | CNY 50–100 per day |
| Group coach guide | CNY 20–30 per person per day |
| Massage therapist | CNY 20–50 |
| Scenic-area porter | CNY 20–50 |

**The ceiling for a normal tourist day is about CNY 100**, and **never tip more than 10% of a bill** — a large tip reads as a mistake or as a foreigner being taken advantage of, and staff will often hand it back.

## The Cash Problem, and Why You Still Need Some

You cannot tip with a foreign card. Chinese card terminals are not configured for a tip line, and a foreign-linked Alipay or WeChat account cannot send red packets (hongbao) or person-to-person transfers — both need a mainland bank account. The tipping budget has to be physical notes.

That is official policy, not a workaround. The State Council's 2024 opinion on improving payment services requires key venues — attractions, hotels, hospitals, transport hubs — to accept mobile payment, bank cards **and** cash, keeps cash as the explicit fallback, and orders a crackdown on businesses that refuse RMB cash ([国办发〔2024〕10号, gov.cn](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/202403/content_6937624.htm)). No one can legally refuse your CNY 20 note.

Withdraw CNY 300–500 at an airport ATM and ask for small denominations. Keep a folded CNY 100 in a separate pocket so you are not opening a full billfold in a hotel lobby.

## Service Charges and Tea Fees Are Not Tips

**服务费 (service charge).** Five-star hotel restaurants, some fine-dining rooms and a few high-end Cantonese places add 10–15%. It goes to the establishment — revenue, not a staff pool — so there is no reason to tip on top. To reward the server specifically, cash is the only way that reaches them.

**茶位费 (tea and seat charge).** Cantonese restaurants, especially in Guangzhou, charge per person for tea and the seat: typically CNY 2–10, more for good tea. It appears even if you only drank water. This is the closest thing China has to a cover charge, and a genuine local convention rather than a tourist trap — the dim sum houses locals eat at, like Diandoude and Taotaoju, charge it too.

## Tipping by City: What Actually Happens

**Beijing.** No tipping at restaurants, none in taxis — Didi charges the exact fare. Porters at five-star hotels in Chaoyang expect CNY 10–20. The main relationship is a private guide: for the Mutianyu Great Wall day, which locals prefer over crowded Badaling, tip at the end. A "guide" at a station selling a cheap Great Wall tour is the commission model the law bans.

**Shanghai.** Service charges are common in Bund and Lujiazui five-star restaurants, so check the bill first. Shengjian bao shops like Dahuchun — ordered by the liang, four pieces — are cash-and-QR counters with no tipping culture.

**Xi'an.** The city-wall bike rental (13.7 km, best at sunset) and the Terracotta Warriors guide are where a tip comes up. If a licensed guide quotes a fee then asks for a tip on top, pay the quoted fee and nothing more. Locals eat in Sajinqiao and Dapiyuan, not the Muslim Quarter.

**Chengdu.** Heming Teahouse in People's Park is the one place a small cash tip is part of the ritual: the ear cleaner charges a set fee, and rounding it up with CNY 10–20 is how it has always worked. Elsewhere — hotpot, per-stick skewer counters, the panda base at 7:30 am — no tip.

**Chongqing and Guilin.** A bowl of xiaomian is CNY 7–10 and the transaction lasts thirty seconds. Guilin rice noodles cost CNY 5–8, paid at the counter before you add your own soup. The people who earn a tip there are Li River raft polers and Longji terrace porters.

**Zhangjiajie.** The park ticket is CNY 228 for four days and the Bailong elevator lifts you 326 metres in a minute; no tipping involved. Porters hauling supplies to the Yuanjiajie viewpoints take cash. The monkeys on the Jinbian Stream trail will take your bag either way.

**Hangzhou, Guangzhou and Shenzhen.** Hangzhou's West Lake hand-rowed boats are the classic tipping moment: the boatman is a private operator, and CNY 20–30 on top of the CNY 150 hourly price is normal. In Guangzhou, yum cha is where the tea fee lives and it is not a tip. Shenzhen, built after 1980 on apps, tips least of all.

## Scams That Use "Tip" as a Cover

Tipping pressure is almost always a symptom of a tour you should not be on.

- **The "free" Great Wall or Forbidden City tour.** Free because the bus stops at a jade factory where the guide earns commission — what Article 35 prohibits.
- **The tea ceremony invitation.** A friendly "student" invites you to a ceremony near Tiananmen or the Bund; the tea costs hundreds of yuan and the host's "tip" is the profit.
- **The rickshaw or "art student" gallery detour.** A small fare, a hard sell, then a tip demanded for the guide's time.
- **The driver who "cannot break a note."** Classic on airport runs. Insist on the meter, or use Didi, where the fare is fixed before you get in.

## If Someone Demands a Tip: How to Complain

Two routes, both free.

**12315** is the national consumer complaint hotline and portal, covering overcharging, refused cash and price fraud ([12315.cn](https://www.12315.cn/)). English service is limited, so a hotel clerk or translation app helps.

**The tourism complaint system.** Ministry of Culture and Tourism rules require the local tourism complaint body to decide within **two working days** whether to accept a case, and set a **90-day** window from the end of your tour contract to file. Complaints run on mediation, and where a guide or agency appears to have broken the law the body must refer it for penalty ([mct.gov.cn](https://zwgk.mct.gov.cn/zfxxgkml/zcfg/bmgz/202602/t20260211_964700.html)).

Photograph the guide's licence badge (they must wear it), keep the contract and receipts, note the date and vehicle plate, and file before you leave. After 90 days the complaint is dead.

## The Short Version

Do not tip at restaurants, in taxis, at bars or in shops — the money will often be handed back, and nobody thinks less of you for paying the bill exactly. Carry CNY 100–200 in small notes for the short list who genuinely expect one: the porter with your bag, the attendant who cleans your room, the guide who gave you a real day, the porter on the mountain steps.

And know the line the law draws. Tipping a guide because you want to is your call. A guide asking for one is a violation carrying a CNY 1,000–10,000 fine, and a "cheap" tour that ends in a jade showroom is the same scheme in a different coat.
