# China Tourist Visa (L Visa) Application Guide 2026: Step-by-Step

China has made entry dramatically easier in recent years — visa-free schemes now cover dozens of nationalities, and transit-without-visa has been extended to 240 hours (10 days) at many ports. But if your passport doesn't qualify for visa-free entry, or you want to stay longer than the scheme allows, you still need the classic **L tourist visa**.

This guide walks through the entire L visa process as it works in 2026: the online form, the documents, the fees, the biometrics, and — most valuably — the mistakes that quietly get applications rejected.

## What the L Visa Actually Is

The **L visa** is China's ordinary tourist visa. It is issued for sightseeing, visiting friends and family, and other non-business, non-study travel. It comes in three flavours:

- **Single entry** — enter China once, stay for the permitted duration, then leave
- **Double entry** — two separate entries within the visa's validity period
- **Multiple entry** — unlimited entries within the validity period (commonly 1, 5, or 10 years depending on nationality)

The two numbers that confuse everyone:

- **Validity** ("enter before") — the deadline by which you must *enter* China. A 10-year US visa means you can make your first entry any time within ten years.
- **Duration of stay** — how long you may remain *per entry* (typically 30, 60, or 90 days). Multiple-entry visas are almost never "unlimited days"; each entry restarts the clock on the permitted stay.

**Check both numbers before you book flights.** A traveller holding a 10-year visa with 60-day stays is still an overstayer on day 61.

## Do You Even Need One?

Before you pay for a visa, check whether you qualify for something faster:

1. **Unilateral visa-free entry** — a growing list of nationalities (including many European countries, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, and South Korea) can enter China for tourism for 15–30 days with no visa at all. The list has expanded several times since 2024 and policies differ by passport.
2. **240-hour visa-free transit** — citizens of 50+ countries, including the US, UK, and Canada, can transit through China for up to 10 days without a visa if travelling onward to a *third* country, and if they stay within the permitted region.
3. **Regional schemes** — Hainan, for example, has its own visa-free arrangements for tour groups and, in some cases, individual travellers from certain countries.

If none of those fit, the L visa is your route. Verify the current rules with your local Chinese embassy or visa application centre before you spend money — this is the single most changeable area of Chinese travel policy.

## Step 1: File the COVA Online Form

Chinese visa applications now start online through the **COVA (China Online Visa Application)** system at *cova.mfa.gov.cn*. It's free to fill in, and it is also where most rejections begin.

Practical notes:

- **Fill it in English**, using exactly the spellings from your passport. A middle name omitted, or a birthdate typed as day/month instead of month/day, can void the application.
- **Every visit counts.** The form asks for the last five years of travel history. Missing a trip that the officer can see in your passport is treated as a misrepresentation.
- **Be specific about your itinerary.** "Tourism, Beijing–Xi'an–Shanghai, 12 days, hotels listed" is strong. "Tourism" alone with no plan looks thin.
- **Save the application number** you receive at the end, plus a PDF of the completed form. You'll print it and sign it — an unsigned form is a same-day rejection.
- **Photograph rules are strict**: 33 mm × 48 mm, white background, no glasses, no uniform, taken within the last six months. The visa centre photo booth costs more than a drugstore photo, but it gets the crop right.

## Step 2: Assemble Your Documents

The standard L visa file, in the order visa centres like to see it:

1. **Passport** — at least six months of remaining validity, at least two blank facing pages, and no damage. A passport with a torn cover or water damage gets bounced.
2. **Photocopy of the passport bio page** and of any previous Chinese visas.
3. **Printed and signed COVA form** with the barcode page.
4. **A compliant photo**, glued or stapled exactly as instructed — some centres require it affixed to the form.
5. **Proof of itinerary** — round-trip flight reservations and hotel bookings covering every night in China. Refundable bookings are fine; consulates care that the plan is coherent, not that it is paid in full.
6. **Proof of legal status** if you're applying in a country where you don't hold citizenship — a residence permit, student visa, or work permit with enough remaining validity.
7. **Former Chinese citizens** — you'll need your previous Chinese passport or a certificate of naturalisation, and you may be asked to surrender an old passport at the visa centre.

**On invitation letters:** for ordinary tourist travel, most consulates now accept itinerary documents (flights and hotels) instead of a formal invitation letter from a Chinese host. Requirements differ between consulates and change often, so download your specific visa centre's checklist rather than relying on a blog — including this one.

## Step 3: Book an Appointment and Fingerprint

In the United States, the UK, Canada, and much of Europe, applications are handled by outsourced **Chinese Visa Application Service Centres (CVASC)** that sit alongside the embassy. You'll typically:

1. Complete the COVA form first — you need its application number to book.
2. Book an appointment slot online. Walk-ins are accepted in some cities but queue times can run to hours.
3. Attend in person for **fingerprinting** if you are aged 14–70 and applying for the first time with that passport. Children under 14, adults 70 and over, and repeat applicants already fingerprinted in the valid window are usually exempt.
4. Pay the fee and collect a **pickup receipt** with a tracking number.

Some countries let you skip the visit entirely by posting your file or using a registered agent. Check whether you qualify before you take a day off work.

## Step 4: Fees and Processing

Fees vary by nationality and consulate, partly because China charges reciprocal rates.

- **US citizens:** approximately **$140** per visa, whether single, double, or multiple entry. This is one of the more expensive rates.
- **Many European and other nationalities:** roughly **$60–$90** for a single-entry tourist visa.
- **Visa centres** often add a **service fee** (commonly $20–$40 in the US, or a comparable local-currency charge elsewhere), which is separate from the embassy fee.
- **Expedited / rush service** typically adds $25–$30 and cuts processing to 2–3 business days.
- **Rush passport return by courier** is a separate add-on in some centres.

**Processing time:** standard is usually **four business days** from the day the centre submits your file to the embassy. In practice, plan for **one to two weeks door to door** once you include appointment availability, mailing of the passport back, and any public holidays. Summer, the weeks around Chinese New Year, and the run-up to Golden Week (early October) are the busiest periods.

**Do not book non-refundable flights before the visa is in your passport.** Review sites are full of travellers who saved $80 on a fare and lost $800 when their visa came back later than expected.

## Step 5: After the Visa Arrives — Two Rules People Break

1. **Check the visa for errors immediately.** Wrong name spelling, wrong passport number, or a duration of stay shorter than you requested — report it to the visa centre the same day. Fixing it later, from inside China, is far harder.
2. **Register your accommodation.** Hotels register foreign guests automatically. If you stay in a private home, a hostel that doesn't handle foreigners, or an Airbnb that can't register you, you are legally required to register at the local police station within 24 hours of arrival. Keep the registration slip.

Also remember: **an L visa is not permission to work, study, or do business.** If you attend meetings for a company based outside China, that's a grey area that the M (business) visa covers properly. Volunteer placements and unpaid internships have tripped up travellers too — check what category your activity actually requires.

## The Mistakes That Get Applications Rejected

- **Incomplete travel history** on the COVA form that contradicts the stamps in your passport.
- **Unsigned or mismatched forms** — the printed form must match the online submission exactly.
- **Thin itinerary** with no hotel nights booked or no return flight.
- **Passport problems** — under six months validity, no blank pages, or visible damage.
- **Wrong photo** — the wrong dimensions, sunglasses, or an old photo that no longer resembles you.
- **Applying in the wrong jurisdiction** — you generally must apply in your country of citizenship or legal residence.
- **Underestimating timing** and losing a non-refundable booking.

## Timeline: When to Start

Work backwards from your departure date:

- **8 weeks out** — confirm whether you need a visa at all; download your visa centre's checklist.
- **6 weeks out** — file the COVA form, gather documents, book the appointment.
- **5 weeks out** — attend the visa centre, pay, start processing.
- **4 weeks out** — collect the passport, verify every printed detail.
- **3 weeks out** — with the visa confirmed, book the flights you actually want.

If you're travelling in peak season, add two weeks to all of the above.

## Concluding

The L visa process is bureaucratic but not mysterious once you see it as a checklist: one online form, one compliant photo, a coherent itinerary, correct fees, and patience with appointment queues. The rejection stories you read online almost always trace back to a detail — an unsigned form, an inconsistent travel history, a passport with five months of validity left.

Check first whether visa-free entry or 240-hour transit already covers your trip. If it doesn't, start the L visa early, apply through the correct visa centre for your jurisdiction, and treat every field on the form as if the officer will cross-check it. They usually do.
