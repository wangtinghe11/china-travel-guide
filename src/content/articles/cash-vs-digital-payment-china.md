# Cash vs Digital Payments in China: What Actually Works in 2026

China is the most cashless large economy on earth, and yet every experienced traveller still arrives with a few hundred yuan in their pocket. That combination confuses first-time visitors: if everything is a QR code, why does anyone bother with notes?

The answer is that digital payments in China are near-universal but not universal, and the gaps are exactly the moments when you have the least capacity to solve a problem — a dead phone at a metro gate, a card your bank froze overnight, a noodle shop whose QR code only accepts Chinese wallets. Cash is not your main payment method. It is your insurance policy, and it costs you nothing to hold.

This guide covers where each method genuinely works, where each one breaks, how much cash to carry, how to get it without getting ripped off on the exchange rate, and what your rights are when a merchant says no.

## The Short Answer

Use Alipay or WeChat Pay for everything routine. Carry CNY 300–500 in cash per person as a buffer, in mixed denominations. Set up both wallets before you fly if you can, because a single wallet is a single point of failure.

That is the whole strategy. Everything below is the detail that makes it work.

## Where Digital Payments Genuinely Win

Almost everywhere money changes hands quickly and at scale:

- **Metro and bus gates.** In Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Chengdu and most other large cities, the gate reads a QR code from Alipay's Transport feature or a transit card inside the app. Some cities also accept a contactless foreign Visa or Mastercard directly at the gate, but coverage is uneven — do not plan around it.
- **Convenience stores.** FamilyMart, 7-Eleven, Lawson, Meixin and the rest are effectively cashless-first. The clerk will usually point at the QR stand before you finish reaching for your wallet.
- **Restaurants.** Ordering is often via a QR code on the table, which opens a mini program menu. You pay inside that mini program, which means a foreign card that works for the merchant's QR stand may not work for the table-order flow.
- **Ride-hailing.** Didi is app-only. There is no cash option to fall back on unless you hail a street taxi.
- **Attractions and museums.** Popular sites increasingly require advance booking through a mini program or official account, paid online. Selling out is common; paying at the gate is often not an option at all.
- **Trains.** Tickets are bought online with a passport number attached, paid digitally, and collected or scanned at the station.

Notice what these have in common: they are not just payments, they are **bookings and access systems**. Digital payment in China is often the entry ticket to a process, not an alternative way to settle a bill. That is why "I'll just use cash" fails at a museum ticket window even though the same cash works fine at the souvenir stall outside.

## Where Digital Payments Break

The failure modes are consistent and worth knowing before they happen:

**The mini program will not take a foreign card.** Plenty of small merchants display only a personal QR code — the kind a Chinese friend transfers to directly. Foreign cards cannot pay personal codes. This is the single most common wall tourists hit, and it appears in exactly the places cash is most useful: markets, family restaurants, temple stalls, rural shops.

**Your bank declines the charge.** Cross-border charges trip fraud systems constantly, particularly on the first transaction of a trip from an unfamiliar country. The fix is in your banking app or a phone call, not in China.

**Your phone dies at the wrong moment.** Navigation, translation, tickets, payment and messaging all live on one device. A dead battery is not an inconvenience in China; it is a full stop. Carry a power bank, and carry cash for the hours it takes to find a charger.

**Verification blocks you on arrival.** New accounts on new devices can require an existing user to confirm you, or a passport check that needs reliable signal. Do this setup at home, not in an arrivals hall.

**No signal, no payment.** Underground car parks, some station basements, mountain scenic areas and rural stretches still have dead zones. Cash does not need signal.

## Where Cash Still Wins

Cash is not just a backup. There are places where it is simply the better tool:

- **Markets and street food.** Vendors appreciate exact change and often have no QR stand at all.
- **Temples and donation boxes.** Still overwhelmingly cash.
- **Small towns and rural areas.** Digital coverage is real but thinner, and personal QR codes dominate.
- **Luggage storage, toilets with an attendant, informal parking.** Small fixed fees, often cash-only in practice.
- **Tipping guides and drivers.** Not customary in China, but if you want to tip a private guide or a driver who went out of their way, cash is the only graceful way to do it — a QR transfer to a personal account is awkward for both sides.
- **When you want to avoid a digital trail.** Some travellers prefer cash for privacy. That is a legitimate choice and it still works.

## The Refusal Question, and Your Rights

Renminbi cash is legal tender in China. The People's Bank of China has stated repeatedly that merchants may not refuse cash, and has run public rectification campaigns against businesses that do — the 2023–2024 rounds specifically targeted retail, catering, parking and scenic-area operators after complaints from elderly and foreign customers.

In practice, distinguish two situations:

1. **"I don't have change."** Extremely common with a CNY 100 note at 8am. This is not refusal, it is logistics. Break large notes at your hotel front desk or a convenience store early in the day, and keep twenties and tens for small purchases.
2. **"We don't take cash."** This is against the rules. Some app-only businesses — unmanned stores, certain coffee chains, some canteens — are structurally incapable of it, and you will not win that argument at the counter. Report persistent refusals to the local branch of the People's Bank of China or via the 12363 financial consumer hotline if you want to push it; otherwise pay digitally and move on.

The practical takeaway: do not plan to be a cash-only traveller in China. You can survive a day on cash, but you cannot run a trip on it, because too many processes are app-mediated.

## How Much Cash, and in What Denominations

CNY 300–500 per person is the right buffer for a city trip. Raise it to CNY 800–1,000 if you are heading to rural areas, hiking regions, or places where you expect to pay small vendors frequently.

Ask for a mix rather than all hundred-yuan notes:

- **CNY 100 × 2** for a real emergency — a taxi across town, a train station meal, a pharmacy.
- **CNY 50 × 2, CNY 20 × 3** for ordinary purchases.
- **CNY 10 and 5 notes** for markets, snacks, temple entries and exact-change situations.

The CNY 100 note is the least useful thing in your wallet on day one, because nobody wants to break it. Spend an hour breaking one at a convenience store and your cash becomes usable.

Also worth knowing: the current series of notes is the 2015-and-later issue with updated security features. If you are handed an older note, it is usually fine — but learn the basic checks (watermark, security thread, raised ink, colour-shifting number) rather than trusting a stranger's reassurance.

## Getting Cash Without Getting Ripped Off

Three rules cover almost every mistake tourists make.

**Rule 1: Withdraw from a bank ATM, not a currency exchange desk at the airport.** Airport exchange counters and hotel desks typically quote rates several percent worse than the interbank rate and add commission. A bank ATM gives you the standard network rate plus a flat fee.

**Rule 2: Always choose to be charged in CNY, never in your home currency.** This is dynamic currency conversion (DCC), and it is the single most expensive mistake in this guide. When the ATM or card terminal offers to convert to your home currency "for your convenience", it is applying its own rate with a markup that commonly runs 3–7%. Decline it. Your own bank's rate is nearly always better even after its foreign transaction fee.

**Rule 3: Withdraw larger amounts less often.** Chinese ATMs commonly charge around CNY 15–30 per foreign-card withdrawal, and your home bank may add a fixed fee plus a percentage. One CNY 2,000 withdrawal beats four CNY 500 withdrawals.

Practical details that save time:

- Look for ATMs with Visa, Mastercard, Cirrus or Plus logos. Bank of China, ICBC, China Construction Bank, Bank of Communications and China Merchants Bank are the reliable options in cities.
- Many ATMs cap a single withdrawal well below your card's limit — if the machine offers less than you asked for, take it and repeat rather than assuming your card failed.
- Use ATMs inside bank branches during business hours when you can. If the machine eats your card or the transaction hangs, there is a human fifty metres away.
- Keep the receipt. If a withdrawal debits but does not dispense, the receipt is your evidence for the dispute.
- Do not rely on a single card. Two cards from two different banks is the standard advice for a reason: fraud systems are aggressive, and a freeze on your only card is a trip-ending event.

## What Cash Cannot Do

Be clear-eyed about the limits, because they are structural rather than incidental:

- **You cannot top up an Alipay or WeChat balance with cash** as a foreigner. Balance top-ups need a mainland Chinese bank card. Your foreign card pays merchants directly; it does not fund a stored balance.
- **You cannot buy train tickets with cash at every station, easily.** Ticket windows exist and accept cash, but inventory is often released online first and the good departures sell out before you reach a counter. Plan trains days ahead, digitally.
- **You cannot book popular attractions on the day with cash.** Many sites are timed-entry and sell out. The booking flow is a mini program.
- **You cannot pay for Didi with cash.** App-based, no cash path.
- **You cannot use cash in an unmanned store.** No cashier, no drawer.

This is the honest picture: cash is essential insurance and a real payment method in markets and small towns, but it is not a parallel economy. Anyone who tells you "just bring cash, you don't need the apps" has not tried to enter the Forbidden City on a Saturday.

## A Sensible Split for a Typical Day

Here is what the balance looks like in practice on a normal city day:

- Breakfast at a local shop: digital, unless it is a hole-in-the-wall with a personal QR code — then cash.
- Metro to a museum: digital at the gate; museum entry booked and paid digitally a day earlier.
- Lunch: digital at the table-order mini program.
- Afternoon market or temple: cash, exact change preferred.
- Taxi home: street cab takes cash; Didi does not.
- Convenience store water: digital, and this is your chance to break a CNY 100 note.

Total cash spent on a typical day: often zero. Total cash that saved the day across a two-week trip: usually one or two incidents — a dead phone, a declined card, a market stall. That is exactly the risk profile CNY 400 is sized for.

## Setup Order That Avoids Most Problems

If you are starting from nothing, do it in this order, at home, before you fly:

1. Install Alipay and WeChat. Register both with your normal mobile number.
2. Complete passport verification in both apps while you have reliable signal and can receive SMS.
3. Link a Visa or Mastercard to each. Use different cards if you have two, so a single freeze does not take out both wallets.
4. Make one small test payment if the app allows it, so you discover verification problems before you are standing at a gate.
5. Order CNY 300–500 in cash from your bank at home, or plan your first ATM withdrawal at the airport bank branch rather than an exchange desk.
6. Pack a power bank and a second payment card.

Do that, and the cash-versus-digital question stops being a decision you have to make under pressure. You will simply pay the way the situation allows, which is what locals do too.

## The Bottom Line

Digital payments are the operating system of daily life in China; cash is the recovery mode. Travellers who arrive with only one of the two spend their trip solving problems that the other would have prevented. Bring both, keep the cash small-denominated and topped up, decline every currency conversion offer, and treat your phone battery as a payment method in its own right.
